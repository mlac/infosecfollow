1. BINANCE TRACES THE $6.43 BILLION HEIST TO FAKE JOB INTERVIEWS
   Ransomware and Cybercrime · [crypto, apt, social-engineering]
   Latest developments: Binance said on November 3, 2026 that
   the record theft of $6.43 billion grew out of a months-long,
   multi-stage campaign: North Korean attackers ran
   recruitment-themed social engineering against two of its
   engineers, planted a tampered Homebrew installation to reach
   their machines, and abused AI tools during the operation,
   replacing the exchange's earlier account of a single
   compromised DevOps laptop; Wired reported that the attackers
   duped an engineer with a fake job interview and a malicious
   download to win the first foothold, and North Korea denied
   responsibility the same day.
   TraderTraitor, the North Korea-aligned crew the FBI, the
   United States Secret Service, and Internal Revenue Service
   Criminal Investigation named on October 31, 2026, drained the
   world's largest cryptocurrency exchange in roughly 20 minutes
   after taking over engineer workstations. Any company whose
   engineers hold production access should treat developer
   package managers and recruiter outreach as attack surface and
   test whether one laptop can reach a withdrawal path.

4. STOLEN CLOUD ACCESS PAYS THE ATTACKER'S AI BILL
   AI Security · [ai, cloud, supply-chain]
   Latest developments: Google Threat Intelligence Group and
   Mandiant disclosed on November 3, 2026 that intruders who
   break into corporate cloud accounts run large language models
   at the victim's expense, a practice they call LLMJacking,
   with single-day charges above $100,000 and total bills
   reaching $1 million across incidents Mandiant handled in 2026
   at financial services and retail companies; Dark Reading
   reported that criminals have built a business out of
   reselling that hijacked model access, and Google traced the
   stolen API keys to phishing, leaked source code,
   misconfigured environments, and exposed client-side code.
   Attackers monetize a stolen cloud credential by billing the
   owner for model inference, which surfaces as a sudden spike
   in API requests. Separately, Glow Labs reviewed real AI
   coding sessions and found agents installing MCP servers from
   archived repositories that will never receive another patch,
   a pattern it names MCPhantom. Cap and alarm on model API
   spend, and pin the MCP packages your agents may install.
